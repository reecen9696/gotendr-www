How can a landscape subcontractor win more tenders?

A landscape subcontractor wins more tenders by tracking win rate properly, bidding only the work it can win, building relationships with head contractor estimators, and submitting compliant prices that are easy to level. Clear scope and clarifications often beat a cheaper price. Ask for feedback after every loss and use it to correct your rates.

What is a tender win rate?

A tender win rate is the number of tenders you win divided by the number you submit, over a set period. It is the simplest measure of whether your estimating effort is turning into work, and the starting point for improving it.

Count it consistently. Decide once whether a tender counts as won when you get a letter of intent, a signed subcontract or a purchase order, and stick to it. Keep invitations you declined out of the calculation, but record them separately, because how often you say no is part of the story.

Do not chase an industry benchmark. There is no reliable figure that suits every subcontractor, and your own trend over time is far more useful than someone else's number.

What should you record for every tender?

You should record enough about every tender to see patterns later, not just whether you won. A simple spreadsheet works. The fields that tend to explain results are:

  1. Head contractor and estimator. Who invited you and who you dealt with.
  2. Project and job type. Residential, education, civil, health, mixed use, and whether it was softscape heavy or hardscape heavy.
  3. Your submitted price. The total, plus the main separable portions.
  4. Hours spent pricing. So you know what each tender cost you to produce.
  5. Number of clarifications and exclusions. A rough measure of how qualified your price was.
  6. Result. Won, lost, or no decision, with the date you found out.
  7. Feedback. Where your price sat, and any reason given.

After a few months this shows which head contractors you actually win with, which job types suit you and where your pricing time is wasted.

Does bidding fewer tenders raise your win rate?

Bidding fewer, better-chosen tenders usually raises your win rate, because the hours go into work you have a real chance of winning. Pricing every invitation spreads your estimating time thin and lowers the quality of every price.

Run each invitation through a quick bid or no-bid check before committing to a full takeoff: scope clarity, competition, fit, contract risk and deadline. Our guide to whether a landscape tender is worth bidding sets out those checks in detail. Your tender records will sharpen the check, because they show which kinds of tender you have historically won.

How do head contractor relationships affect winning?

Head contractor relationships affect winning because estimators prefer subcontractors whose prices they trust and whose work they have seen. When prices are close, a known and reliable subcontractor is the safer choice for the person who has to recommend one.

Relationships are built through behaviour, not lunches. Respond to every invitation, even with a polite decline. Submit on time. Answer queries quickly. Deliver what you priced on the jobs you win, and keep your scope consistent from one tender to the next so the estimator knows what your number includes.

How do you make a tender easy to level?

A tender is easy to level when the head contractor can compare it line by line with the other landscape prices without chasing you. Tender levelling is the process of adjusting each subcontractor's price onto a common scope, and a price that is hard to level is often adjusted against you or set aside.

  • Price in the head contractor's format or bill of quantities if one is provided.
  • Break the price into the separable portions and trade packages they ask for.
  • Price every PC item and provisional sum exactly as stated.
  • Acknowledge every addendum by number.
  • Keep clarifications and exclusions short, numbered and specific.

A priced bill of quantities with clear line items is the easiest kind of price to level, and the hardest to misread. The documents needed to price a landscape tender guide covers what to check is in the package before you start.

Is a lower price the only way to win?

A lower price is not the only way to win, and it is the most expensive one. Many landscape tenders are lost on scope gaps, long lists of exclusions or qualifications the head contractor cannot accept, rather than on the bottom line alone.

Read the scope closely, price what it actually asks for, and write clarifications that remove doubt rather than shift risk wholesale. A sharp, lightly qualified price can beat a cheaper one full of exclusions, because the head contractor has to add the excluded work back in when levelling. Our guides to reading a landscape tender scope and writing tender clarifications and exclusions cover both sides.

When should you follow up a tender?

You should follow up a tender twice: once to confirm it was received, and again around the time the head contractor is letting the landscape package. Head contractors often let landscaping well after they win the main contract, so a tender can sit quiet for a long time.

Ask the estimator when they expect to decide rather than guessing. When you follow up, bring something useful, such as a confirmed lead time, an alternative product or an answer to an open query. A follow-up that only asks whether a decision has been made adds little.

How do you get feedback after losing?

You get feedback after losing by asking for it directly, briefly and every time. Call or email the estimator once the package has been let and ask two questions: roughly where your price sat, and whether anything in your scope or exclusions counted against you.

Many estimators will not share other subcontractors' prices, and that is fair. A broad answer, such as close, mid-pack or well out, is still valuable. Record whatever you get in your tender log, and thank them, because the next invitation often comes from the same person.

How do you use debriefs to correct your rates?

A post-tender debrief is a short internal review that compares your price against the feedback and your own costs, then corrects the rates that caused the gap. It turns every lost tender into better pricing on the next one.

  1. Gather the facts. Your priced bill, the feedback, and the scope as tendered.
  2. Find the big lines. Identify the few items that make up most of your price.
  3. Check quantities first. A measurement error can look like a rate problem.
  4. Check rates against actuals. Compare the rates for those items against costs from jobs you have completed.
  5. Check overhead and margin. See whether the gap sits in direct costs or in what you added on top.
  6. Update the rate library. Change the rate, note why and date it.

Do this for wins too. A tender won by a wide margin can mean a rate is too low, which costs money on every job until it is fixed. The guide to pricing overhead and margin covers the last two steps in more depth.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good tender win rate for a landscape subcontractor?

There is no reliable benchmark worth quoting, because win rate depends on how selectively you bid, who you bid for and how you count. The useful comparison is against your own past results, split by head contractor and job type, tracked consistently over time.

Should I count tenders I declined in my win rate?

Record them, but keep them out of the win rate itself. Win rate should be tenders won divided by tenders submitted. Declined invitations belong in a separate count, because they show how selective you are and which head contractors keep sending work.

How do I ask a head contractor for feedback on a lost tender?

Call or email the estimator once the trade is let and ask two plain questions: roughly where your price sat against the others, and whether anything in your scope or exclusions counted against you. Keep it short and thank them for any answer.

Will cutting my price win more tenders?

Sometimes, but it is the most expensive way to win. Many tenders are lost on scope gaps, heavy exclusions or a submission that is hard to compare, not on price alone. Fix those first, then check your rates against what your feedback tells you.

When should I follow up a submitted landscape tender?

Confirm receipt shortly after submitting, then follow up around the time the head contractor is likely to be letting the landscape package. Ask the estimator when they expect to decide rather than guessing. Follow up with something useful, not just a reminder.

What is a post-tender debrief?

A post-tender debrief is a short internal review after a tender is won or lost. You compare your price and scope against any feedback received, note which rates or allowances looked out, and update your rate library so the next tender starts from better figures.

Key takeaways

  • Win rate is tenders won divided by tenders submitted; count it the same way every time.
  • Log head contractor, job type, price, pricing hours, result and feedback for every tender.
  • Bid fewer, better-chosen tenders so each price gets the time it needs.
  • Make your submission easy to level: their format, every item priced, numbered clarifications.
  • Sharp scope and light qualifications often beat a cheaper, heavily excluded price.
  • Ask for feedback after every result and use debriefs to correct your rate library.
