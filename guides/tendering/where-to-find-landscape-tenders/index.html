Where do you find landscape tenders in Australia?

Landscape tenders in Australia are found on EstimateOne, where builders invite subcontractors to price landscape packages; on tender aggregators such as Australian Tenders, illion TenderLink and Tenders.net; on AusTender for Commonwealth work; on each state government portal; on council websites and e-tendering portals; and through direct invitations from builders you already work with.

What kinds of landscape tenders are there?

Landscape tenders come from two different worlds, and each is found in a different place. Knowing which world a job sits in tells you where to look, who you are dealing with and what the submission will need.

A public landscape tender is work put out by a government agency or a council. It is often landscape construction, streetscapes, parks and open space, or a grounds maintenance contract, and it is usually advertised on a government or council portal with formal returnable documents.

A private landscape tender is a request from a head contractor, usually a builder, for subcontractors to price the landscape package on a project. Most of this work moves through EstimateOne, with the rest arriving as direct invitations from builders the subcontractor already knows.

How do you find private landscape tenders on EstimateOne?

EstimateOne, often called E1, is an Australian construction tendering platform that connects head contractors with subcontractors and suppliers. When a builder is tendering a project, it uses E1 to send out trade packages, including the landscape package, and to collect subcontractor prices.

E1 has a landscaping tenders category where you can browse projects that include landscape work. Set up your company profile with your trade, the regions you work in and the size of job you take on, so builders can find you and invite you directly.

For many commercial landscape subcontractors, E1 is the main source of work. The same project is often tendered by several builders at once, so you may receive the same drawings from more than one head contractor and need to price each one against that builder's documents and conditions.

What do tender aggregators do?

A tender aggregator is a service that collects public tenders from many government and council sources and republishes them in one place, sorted by category and region. The main ones are Australian Tenders, illion TenderLink and Tenders.net.

The value of an aggregator is coverage and alerts. Rather than checking dozens of portals and council websites yourself, you set a category such as landscaping and a region, and the service sends you matching tenders. These alerts are typically a paid service, so check each provider for what is included.

An aggregator points you to the tender; it does not replace the source. You still register and download the documents on the issuing portal, and you lodge your response wherever the tender documents say to lodge it.

Where are government landscape tenders advertised?

AusTender is where Australian Government (Commonwealth) tenders are published. Commonwealth landscape work is less common than state and council work, but it is worth a saved search if you operate near Commonwealth sites.

In Victoria, state government tenders are advertised on the Buying for Victoria Tenders Portal, formerly TendersVIC, where suppliers register to view and respond. Parks Victoria advertises its open tenders there, and some state work runs through registers and panels you apply to join. Our guide to landscape tenders in Victoria covers it in detail.

In New South Wales, government opportunities are published on buy.nsw, which absorbed the old NSW eTendering system and has listed grounds maintenance and turf management tenders. The guide to landscape tenders in NSW explains how it works alongside council portals.

Every other state and territory also has its own government tender portal, so register on the portal for each state you are prepared to work in.

How do councils advertise landscape tenders?

Councils are one of the biggest sources of public landscape tenders, covering parks, reserves, streetscapes, sporting grounds and maintenance contracts. Council work is generally not on the state government portal, so it needs its own search.

Councils advertise on their own websites and through e-tendering portals such as TenderLink, VendorPanel and eProcure, depending on the council. The practical approach is to list the councils within your working area and check which portal each one uses, then register on each.

Councils also run panels. A panel is a pre-approved group of suppliers that a council calls on for work over a set period, for example a civil works panel covering hard and soft landscaping and streetscapes. Getting onto a panel can open work that is never advertised as a single tender.

Why do builder relationships matter?

A direct invitation from a builder you already work with is often the best landscape tender you will get. The estimator knows your work, the field is usually smaller, and there is a relationship behind the price.

Builders keep lists of subcontractors they trust to price on time and deliver. The way onto those lists is simple and slow: respond to every invitation, even with a polite decline, submit clean and complete prices by the deadline, and ask estimators to include you on future projects.

How do you set up tender alerts?

A tender alert is a saved search that emails you when a new tender matches your criteria. Alerts are how you stop landscape tenders slipping past, because public tenders often have short windows and are easy to miss if you rely on checking portals by hand.

  1. List every channel relevant to your area: E1, AusTender, your state portal, and each council you would work for.
  2. Register as a supplier on each one, using the same business details everywhere.
  3. Set saved searches by category: landscaping, grounds maintenance, streetscape and open space.
  4. Narrow each search by region so you only see work you can realistically deliver.
  5. Send alerts to a shared inbox so tenders are not lost when someone is on site or on leave.
  6. Review the list regularly and drop searches that only ever return work you pass on.

Which landscape tenders should you bid?

Finding landscape tenders is the easy part; choosing which to price is where the money is made or lost. Pricing takes hours, so every tender should pass a quick bid or no-bid filter first: scope clarity, competition, fit, risk and deadline. Our guide on whether a landscape tender is worth bidding walks through each check.

Public tenders add their own qualifying hurdles. They often require returnable schedules, evidence of insurances, WHS documentation, and sometimes prequalification or panel membership. Check the tender documents early, because a missing requirement can rule you out regardless of price.

How do you price a landscape tender once you find it?

Pricing a landscape tender starts with reading the scope, then measuring the drawings, then applying your rates. Read the specification and conditions first so you know what is included and excluded; our guide to reading a landscape tender scope covers what to look for.

Next comes the quantity takeoff, which measures every item from the drawings into a bill of quantities. Your own rates are then applied to each line, with preliminaries and margin added, as set out in how to price a commercial landscape tender.

Frequently asked questions

Where are most commercial landscape tenders advertised?

Private commercial landscape packages are mostly sent out by builders through EstimateOne or by direct invitation. Public landscape tenders from government and councils are advertised on AusTender, state government portals, council websites and council e-tendering portals, and are republished by aggregators.

What is the difference between a public and a private landscape tender?

A public tender is issued by a government agency or council, usually direct to contractors, with formal returnable schedules. A private tender is issued by a head contractor asking subcontractors to price the landscape package on a building project they are tendering or delivering.

Do I need to pay to find landscape tenders?

Not always. Government portals and council websites can be searched once you register, and builder invitations cost nothing. Aggregators such as Australian Tenders, illion TenderLink and Tenders.net typically charge for alerts, so check each provider for what is free and what is paid.

How do I get invited to more builder tenders?

Keep an up-to-date subcontractor profile on EstimateOne, respond to every invitation even when you decline, submit clean and complete prices on time, and ask the estimators you work with to add you to their landscape list for future projects.

What are council landscape panels?

A panel is a pre-approved group of suppliers a council or agency calls on for work over a set period. Once on a panel, you may be asked to quote on jobs that are never advertised publicly, so watch for panel tenders as well as single projects.

Does Tendr list landscape tenders?

No. Tendr does not list or resell tenders. You find the tender through your usual channels, and Tendr prices it: the quantity takeoff, a priced bill of quantities against your own rates, and the tender submission, for a fixed fee quoted up front.

Key takeaways

  • Landscape tenders come from two worlds: public work from government and councils, and private packages from builders.
