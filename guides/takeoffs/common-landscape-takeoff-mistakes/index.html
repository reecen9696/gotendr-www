What are the most common landscape takeoff mistakes?

The most common landscape takeoff mistakes are measuring at the wrong scale, pricing a superseded revision, missing the specification, and miscounting plants against the schedule. Close behind are double-counted areas, scope hidden in details or civil drawings, volume errors, no wastage, unrecorded assumptions and unread addenda. Each one loses a winnable tender or wins a job at a loss.

Why do takeoff mistakes lose landscape tenders?

A quantity takeoff is the measurement of every item of work from the tender drawings and specification, recorded as quantities that rates are applied to. If the quantities are wrong, the price is wrong, however good your rates are.

Takeoff errors cut both ways. Over-measuring pushes your price above the field and loses tenders you should have won. Under-measuring or missing scope wins the job, then the margin disappears on site, and the error cannot be claimed back because it was yours.

The mistakes below are the ones that come up again and again. For the full method, see how to do a landscape quantity takeoff.

Is the drawing at the scale you think it is?

Measuring at the wrong scale happens when a PDF has been printed to a different sheet size, a viewport is at a different scale from the title block, or the file was never scaled at all. Every area and length on that sheet is then wrong by the same factor, often without looking obviously off.

To catch it, calibrate every sheet against a known dimension, not the title block alone. Check a written dimension or a scale bar before measuring, and re-check any enlarged plan or detail separately, since those are usually drawn at a different scale to the general arrangement.

Are you measuring the current revision?

Tender packages are often reissued, and an old revision can sit in the same folder as the new one. Measuring a superseded drawing means pricing work that has changed, moved or been deleted, and the head contractor will compare your price to bidders who used the right set.

Before measuring, check every sheet against the drawing register or transmittal and confirm the revision letter matches. Record the revision you measured against each sheet in your takeoff, so anyone reviewing it can see exactly what the price is based on.

Have you read the specification, not just the plans?

The specification is the written document that defines materials, depths, methods and standards. Plans show where things go; the spec usually says how deep the topsoil is, what edging is required, how the subgrade must be prepared and what the mulch must be. A takeoff from plans alone misses all of that.

The cost is scope you have not priced: cultivation, ripping, subgrade treatment, specified edging and imported soil depths that differ from what you assumed. Read the landscape spec clause by clause and tick off each one against a line in your takeoff. Our guide to reading a landscape tender scope covers this in detail.

Do your plant counts match the planting schedule?

A planting schedule is the table listing each species, its pot size and the total quantity. It frequently disagrees with the symbols on the plan, because one was updated and the other was not, or because mass planting is shown as a density rather than individual symbols.

Pricing the schedule total without counting the plan, or the reverse, means either missing plants or pricing plants that are not there. Count the plan yourself, calculate density areas at the specified spacing, and reconcile against the schedule species by species. Where they differ, raise a tender query and state which figure you priced.

Are overlapping areas being counted twice?

Double-counting happens when layered areas overlap: a garden bed measured for mulch that is also inside a turf area, paving measured over a zone already taken as soil, or a tree pit counted inside the surrounding bed. Each overlap inflates the quantity and the price.

It is a quiet way to lose a tender, because the price looks reasonable until someone else comes in lower. Measure each surface type as its own layer, check that the layers add up to no more than the site area, and look at the takeoff markup visually for shapes that sit on top of each other.

What items are shown only in the details?

Many landscape items appear only in construction details and sections, never on the general arrangement plan. Common ones are edge restraints, root barriers, tree pit build-ups, subsoil drainage, geotextile, tree stakes and ties, and mowing strips.

These items are easy to miss and costly when you do, because they run the length of every bed or sit under every tree. Go through every detail sheet and ask where each detail applies on the plan, then measure it there. A detail that is referenced nowhere on the plan is itself a tender query.

Is landscape scope hiding in civil or architectural drawings?

Landscape scope does not always sit in the landscape set. Civil drawings can carry batter planting, swale treatments, subsoil drainage and bulk earthworks levels. Architectural drawings can carry planter boxes, podium build-ups, feature walls and paving that the landscape package is expected to supply.

Ignoring them leaves a gap the head contractor assumed you covered. Read the scope of works to see which trade owns each item, then skim the civil, architectural and hydraulic drawings for anything landscape-related. Our list of documents needed to price a landscape tender helps confirm you have them all.

Are your volumes calculated correctly?

Volume is area multiplied by depth, and the most common error is units: multiplying square metres by a depth in millimetres without converting to metres first. A 300 mm depth is 0.3 m, so the volume is area x 0.3, and 1 m3 equals 1,000 L when converting to bagged or bulk product.

The second error is ignoring compaction. Soil and some mulches settle after placement, so the loose volume ordered is more than the compacted volume shown on the drawing. Take the compaction or bulking factor from the specification, your supplier or your own job records. Our guide to calculating topsoil, mulch and turf quantities works through the method.

Have you allowed for wastage where the spec is silent?

Wastage is the material lost to cutting, breakage, offcuts and handling that never ends up in the finished work. Turf rolls are cut to curves, pavers are cut at edges and bulk material is lost in spreading. Pricing the net drawn quantity alone means buying more than you priced.

Measure the net quantity first, then apply wastage as a separate, visible factor. Where the specification sets one, use it. Where it is silent, take the factor from your supplier or your own records for similar work, and state it in your assumptions.

Are your assumptions and exclusions written down?

Every takeoff involves judgement calls: which depth applies where the spec is vague, which document governs when two disagree, what is excluded because it is unclear. An assumption that stays in the estimator's head is worthless the moment the job is awarded.

Unrecorded assumptions turn legitimate variations into your cost, because there is nothing to show the scope you priced. Keep an assumptions and exclusions list as you measure, not at the end, and submit it with the tender as qualifications. A scope and contract review can check that list against the contract before you submit.

Have you read every addendum?

An addendum is a formal change to the tender documents issued during the tender period. Addenda can add scope, delete areas, reissue drawings or answer another bidder's query in a way that changes your price. Missing one means your price is based on documents that no longer apply.

Log every addendum as it arrives, note which drawings and clauses it affects, and re-measure those items. Before submitting, acknowledge each addendum by number in your tender, so it is clear your price includes them.

How do you check a takeoff before you submit?

A takeoff QA check is a separate pass over the finished takeoff, ideally by someone who did not measure it. Use the table below as the checklist, then carry the checked quantities into your bill of quantities.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common quantity takeoff mistake?

Measuring from the wrong information is the most common and the most expensive: a drawing at the wrong scale, a superseded revision, or a plan read without its specification. Every quantity that follows inherits the error, so check scale, revision and spec before you measure anything.

Why do landscapers lose tenders they should win?

Often because the takeoff was wrong, not the rates. Over-measured areas, double-counted zones and padded quantities push the price above the field, while missed scope wins the job at a loss. An accurate, well-qualified takeoff is what lets a good rate actually win.

How do you check a landscape takeoff before submitting?

Run a QA pass separate from the measuring: confirm scale and revision on every sheet, reconcile plant counts against the schedule, tick off every detail and spec clause, check depth units and volumes, and read every addendum. A second person catches more than the original estimator.

Should wastage be included in a takeoff?

Measure net quantities from the drawings, then apply wastage as a separate, visible factor where the spec is silent. Take the factor from your supplier or your own job records, and state it as an assumption so the head contractor can see how the quantity was built.

What should you do when the drawings and schedule disagree?

Raise it as a tender query before the deadline. If there is no answer in time, price the quantity you believe is correct, state which document you followed, and record the discrepancy in your qualifications so it becomes a variation rather than your loss.

Key takeaways

  • Wrong quantities make a wrong price, however good your rates are.
  • Check scale, revision and specification before measuring anything.
  • Reconcile plant counts with the schedule and measure every detail where it applies.
  • Convert depths to metres, and take compaction and wastage factors from the spec, supplier or your records.
  • Write assumptions down as you go and submit them as qualifications.
  • Read, re-measure and acknowledge every addendum.
